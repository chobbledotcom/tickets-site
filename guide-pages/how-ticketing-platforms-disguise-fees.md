    content: |
      Ticketing platforms usually publish their fees on a pricing page. The
      number on the page is not always the number an organiser or attendee
      pays, because the charge can be split across two fees, attached to a
      donation, shifted onto the buyer, or described as flat when its share of
      a cheap ticket is large.

      This guide sets out the patterns the reviewed providers' published pages
      show. Every figure comes from a dated comparison page on this site, and
      each section links to the page that records its source.

      A discount off a charge can still leave the charge higher than a platform
      that never makes it. The check throughout is the total charge, who pays
      it, and what funds any pledge attached to it.

      ## A free-events floor is not a low rate

      Most platforms on the comparison list charge nothing on free events. The
      same platforms charge a percentage plus a fixed amount on each paid
      ticket. [Eventbrite](/compared-to/eventbrite/) charges 6.95% + £0.59 per
      paid ticket in the UK, [Humanitix](/compared-to/humanitix/) 4.67% + £0.46,
      [Ticketpass](/compared-to/ticketpass/) 3.8% + £0.40 on Essentials, and
      [TicketSource](/compared-to/ticketsource/) 7% per paid booking.

      A headline that pairs "free events free" with those paid rates describes
      a floor of zero, not a low rate on paid tickets. The paid rate is what an
      event that sells tickets pays. [Tito](/compared-to/tito/),
      [TixFox](/compared-to/tixfox/) and [Weezevent](/compared-to/weezevent/)
      also charge nothing on free events and a fee on every paid one.

      The zero figure states the cost of a free event. It does not state the
      cost of the event that sells tickets.

      ## Two fees at the same rate

      [Ticketpass](/compared-to/ticketpass/) publishes two charges on each
      Essentials ticket at the same rate: a booking fee of 1.9% + £0.20 and a
      service fee of 1.9% + £0.20. Together they are 3.8% + £0.40, but
      "1.9% + £0.20" is the figure that headlines the page.

      Ticketpass's Give Back 50 pledge donates up to 50% of the booking fee, not
      the service fee. On a £15 ticket the booking fee is about £0.49 and the
      service fee about £0.49; the pledge donates up to about £0.24, and the
      service fee sits outside it.

      A single rate named twice is one charge in two parts. Reading the booking
      fee alone understates what the ticket carries by the amount of the
      service fee.

      ## A pledge funded by the charge on the ticket

      Two providers on the list direct part of what they charge to a cause.
      [Ticketpass](/compared-to/ticketpass/) donates up to 50% of its booking
      fee; [Humanitix](/compared-to/humanitix/) donates 100% of its profits.
      Both are funded by the fee charged on the ticket, not by the platform's
      own income.

      Across 500 £15 tickets (one per payment, before VAT), Ticketpass
      Essentials charges about £485 and donates up to about £121 of it. The
      same 500 tickets cost about £263 on [Chobble Tickets](/features/no-per-ticket-fees/):
      £50 hosting plus about £212 in Stripe processing. The £222 difference,
      donated directly to the same charity, is about £100 more than the pledge
      sends, and the organiser still spends less.

      Ticketpass's own terms describe the pledge as "not a binding contractual
      obligation but a commitment". Humanitix donates 100% of profits rather
      than a share of the fee, so the amount depends on what the company counts
      as cost before profit. The [where ticketing money goes](/guide/choosing-a-ticket-platform/where-ticketing-money-goes/)
      guide sets the pledges beside the charges that fund them.

      A reduced charity rate is a discount on a charge, not its removal.
      Humanitix's charity rate of 3.33% + £0.25 and Ticket Tailor's 50%
      charity discount both still charge on each paid ticket. A lower rate
      funds the donation the same way the standard rate does.

      ## Flat fees that are large percentages

      A charge described as a fixed amount per ticket does not change with the
      ticket price, so its share of the price rises as the price falls.
      [Ticket Tailor](/compared-to/ticket-tailor/) charges 60p per paid ticket
      pay-as-you-go; on a £3 stay-and-play ticket that is 20% of the price.

      [TixFox](/compared-to/tixfox/) charges 38p, or 30p on tickets under £5;
      the 30p rate is 10% of a £3 ticket. [Weezevent](/compared-to/weezevent/)
      charges 2.5% + £0.99, and the 99p fixed portion alone is about a fifth of
      a £5 ticket.

      [Ticket Monkey](/compared-to/ticketmonkey/) charges 25p per paid ticket
      including VAT, which is still about 8% of a £3 ticket.

      "Flat" describes the shape of the charge, not its size. On a £50 ticket
      the same 60p is 1.2%; on a £2 ticket it is 30%. A per-ticket fee that
      looks small next to an expensive ticket can be the larger share of a
      cheap one.

      ## Free for organisers when the buyer pays

      [FIXR](/compared-to/fixr/) is free for organisers because ticket buyers
      pay 4.99% + £0.49 per ticket plus VAT, with a £1 minimum.
      [Resident Advisor](/compared-to/resident-advisor/) says promoters pay
      nothing because buyers cover a service fee.
      [Ticketebo](/compared-to/ticketebo/) is free for organisers because
      buyers pay 4.95% (incl. VAT) per transaction, with a 75p minimum. The
      organiser can choose to absorb it instead.

      The total cost of selling a ticket is not lower because the buyer is
      charged instead of the organiser. The buyer pays a fee above the
      underlying card-processing cost, and the organiser chooses whether to
      absorb it or pass it on.

      A headline written for organisers ("free for organisers") and a headline
      written for buyers ("service fee £x") describe the same transaction from
      two sides. Quoting the one that reads lower does not change what the
      ticket carries.

      ## A zero floor with requested contributions

      [Dandelion](/compared-to/dandelion/) charges no subscription and no
      mandatory fee on tickets sold through the platform. It normally asks
      organisers for 1% of completed sales, asks buyers for a donation at
      checkout, charges £25 for events that send buyers elsewhere, and sells
      paid boosts that raise an event in public listings.

      The advertised 0% is the floor, not the expected cost. Whether the
      organiser pays depends on contributions the platform records as
      voluntary. A "no fee" platform can still take a share of sales through a
      request the organiser is expected to meet.

      ## Affiliate, rep and boost schemes

      Several reviewed providers run schemes that pay a third party when a
      ticket is sold. [FIXR](/compared-to/fixr/) has a rep and affiliate
      network where promoters sell tickets on the organiser's behalf and earn
      commission. [Humanitix](/compared-to/humanitix/) issues shareable
      affiliate codes, and [Dandelion](/compared-to/dandelion/) sells listing
      boosts.

      A promoter earns commission on tickets sold through their link. An
      attendee who buys through a rep's code pays the same price as one who
      does not, so the code rewards the promoter rather than reducing the fee
      on the ticket. A "deal" reached through an affiliate link is an ordinary
      sale that pays the promoter, not a price cut funded by the platform.

      Visibility on a marketplace can be paid as well as earned. Dandelion's
      boosts and Ticket Tailor's [Discover](/compared-to/ticket-tailor/) area
      place paid positioning beside organic listings, and the reader cannot
      tell them apart from the listing alone.

      ## Bundled processing that hides the margin

      Some providers quote a single fee that includes payment processing.
      [Humanitix](/compared-to/humanitix/), [EVENTIM.Light](/compared-to/eventim-light/),
      [Ticketebo](/compared-to/ticketebo/) and the [Ticketpass](/compared-to/ticketpass/)
      booking fee each fold the card cost into the platform charge.

      A bundled figure does not show how much of it is the platform's margin
      and how much is the card-processing cost. [Chobble Tickets](/features/no-per-ticket-fees/)
      separates £50 a year for hosting from Stripe's 1.5% + 20p, so the
      platform take and the processing cost read separately.

      Many of these figures are also quoted excluding VAT. Ticketpass and
      Humanitix both exclude VAT from their headline rates, so a price
      compared on the headline alone is 20% lower than the VAT-inclusive
      charge. Add 20% to compare like-for-like.

      ## The subscription you also need

      A headline ticketing fee can sit on top of a subscription the headline
      omits. [Wix Events](/compared-to/wix-events/) adds a 2.5% service fee to
      paid tickets and requires a Wix Business & eCommerce website plan,
      priced from about £276 a year, plus 2.1% + 20p in Wix Payments
      processing.

      Self-hosted software quoted as free can carry a similar catch.
      [Hi.Events](/compared-to/hi-events/) is free under the AGPL but shows a
      "Powered by Hi.Events" notice unless a $499 commercial licence removes
      it.

      The ticketing fee and the prerequisite subscription are both costs of
      selling tickets on that platform. Quoting one without the other
      understates the total.

      ## Comparison tables the platform writes

      Fee comparison tables published by platforms tend to quote current
      figures for themselves and out-of-date figures for everyone else. That
      observation, and the undisclosed promotion it sits beside, is on the

      ## Prices that move

      A signed-up rate is not a locked rate. [Eventbrite](/compared-to/eventbrite/)'s
      pricing has changed multiple times over the years, generally trending
      upward, and a platform that changes operator can change terms with it.

      [Ticketpass](/compared-to/ticketpass/) changed operator on 9 October
      2025, when Fanex Group Ltd acquired the business. Fanex Group Ltd lists
      one person holding 75% or more of its shares, who is also the executive
      chairman of the investment firm Liberté Capital. Ticketpass's terms
      describe the Give Back 50 pledge as a commitment rather than a binding
      obligation.

      What limits a change is the structure underneath the price. [Chobble
      Tickets](/about/) is a community interest company limited by guarantee
      with no share capital, so control cannot be acquired by buying a stake.
      Its code is published under AGPL-3.0-only, so an organiser can run the
      same product on another host.

      ## Questions to compare

      - What is the total charge on a typical ticket, including every fee, VAT
        and payment processing?
      - Who pays that charge, the organiser or the attendee?
      - Does the platform charge separately on free events and paid events,
        and what is the paid rate?
      - If there is a charity pledge, is it a share of the booking fee, of all
        fees, or of profit?
      - Is a flat fee a small share of a cheap ticket, or a large one?
      - Is the rate locked, and could a change of operator change it?
      - What subscription, licence or branding requirement sits underneath the
        headline?

      guide lists the published rates and pledges behind these patterns. The
